\subsection{Obsolete states change decisions}
\label{sec:results-h1}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/constructions.pdf}
\caption{Decision effects by construction (confirmation, 384 histories per task, 95\% history-bootstrap
intervals). (a) $D_c$: increase in the wrong action's log-odds when the edited word implies the wrong
rather than the correct action. (b) Net generated switches toward the wrong action per history.}
\label{fig:constructions}
\end{figure*}

H1 is supported in both tasks. When the superseded state implied the wrong action rather than the
correct one, the wrong action's log-odds rose by $D_{\mathrm{superseded}} = 3.904$ nats in access
(97.5\% interval $[3.474, 4.346]$) and $3.133$ $[2.743, 3.516]$ in routing. For scale, editing the
\emph{current} state itself, which legitimately changes the correct action, moved the same quantity by
$-10.100$ and $-8.197$ nats.

The shift reaches generated answers. The net switch rate toward the wrong action was $0.167$
$[0.130, 0.206]$ in access and $0.185$ $[0.143, 0.227]$ in routing. Switches were nearly one-directional:
65 histories switched to the wrong action and 1 to the correct one in access, 75 and 4 in routing. In
each competent routing cell, the net switch rate was between 0.164 and 0.227 (Table~\ref{tab:switch-cells}).
Figure~\ref{fig:competence} shows this as accuracy. In every routing cell, decisions were correct
in 0.906--0.953 of histories when the superseded state implied the correct action, and in 0.727--0.750
when it implied the wrong one. The same records gave current- and initial-state retrieval at or above
0.98.

\subsection{The interference is not specific to supersession}
\label{sec:results-h2}

H2 compares the superseded assignment with an assignment of the same word to a different attribute that
was later updated. It is supported in routing, $\Delta_{\mathrm{semantic}} = +1.224$ $[0.973, 1.485]$, and
inconclusive in access, $+0.035$ $[-0.318, 0.400]$. The generated switches agree: $+0.120$
$[0.076, 0.164]$ in routing, $+0.029$ $[-0.016, 0.073]$ in access. Whether the word was assigned to the
queried attribute therefore mattered in routing but was not detectable in access.

The mention control, a secondary outcome, gives the clearer answer. When the same entity only
\emph{mentioned} the word, the decision effect was larger than for the superseded assignment:
$D_{\mathrm{entity\,mention}} = 5.182$ against $3.904$ in access and $4.807$ against $3.133$ in routing
(Figure~\ref{fig:constructions}a). That gives $\Delta_{\mathrm{mention}} = -1.278$ $[-1.579, -0.977]$ and
$-1.675$ $[-1.896, -1.441]$. In generated answers, mentions caused at least as many switches as
superseded assignments: $0.195$ (76 to wrong, 1 to correct) against $0.167$ in access, an inconclusive
difference, and $0.294$ (113 against 0) against $0.185$ in routing, difference $-0.109$
$[-0.156, -0.065]$. A word attached to no entity still shifted preference substantially, by $3.043$ and
$2.496$ nats, but rarely changed a generated decision ($0.062$ and $0.039$). Score shifts of similar size
can thus have very different behavioral consequences.

\paragraph{Replication across splits.} The same protocol family was also run on four earlier splits: the
development and gate splits of the first redesign and of the confirmed protocol. These used the
standard template, and the first redesign kept the \lit{Answer:} line. They are not confirmatory, but
they show which conclusions depend on wording (Figure~\ref{fig:replication} in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).
$D_{\mathrm{superseded}}$ was positive in all ten split-by-task estimates, between 3.133 and 6.599 nats.
$\Delta_{\mathrm{semantic}}$ was positive with an interval excluding zero in every estimate except the
confirmation's access task. $\Delta_{\mathrm{mention}}$ never favored the superseded assignment. On the
standard template, however, it ranged from $-0.806$ to $+0.163$, and six of eight intervals included zero.
On that template, mention effects were indistinguishable from or larger than superseded effects. On the
held-out template, they were clearly larger. No split
showed a superseded assignment exceeding the mention control. The size of the mention advantage, however,
depends on wording.

The edit strongly affected easy retrieval scores: the mean $R_{\mathrm{easy}}$ on superseded items was
$8.096$ nats in access and $10.878$ in routing. That sensitivity carried no information about which
decisions would fail (Table~\ref{tab:h3}). Pre-decision features reduced log loss from $0.414$ to
$0.346$ and reached AUROC $0.780$. Adding $R_{\mathrm{easy}}$ changed log loss by $+0.0004$ nats,
interval $[-0.0011, 0.0019]$; the upper end is under 3\% of what the simple features already gain. The
result is the same in each task and for the wrong-answer outcome. Within this design,
$R_{\mathrm{easy}}$ describes how retrieval scores respond to the edit; it does not diagnose decision
vulnerability.

\subsection{A second model did not meet the competence gate}
\label{sec:results-gemma}

Gemma 3 4B \citep{gemma2025gemma3} was added under a model list frozen before any of its data were scored,
with the same rows, rules and runtime. The only change was its end-of-turn event, corrected after a
format-only smoke test (Appendix~\ref{app:history}). On the gate split, no cell met the criteria.
Current-only decision accuracy was 0.609--0.719, direct retrieval 0.916--0.964, and decision accuracy
with history 0.488--0.678. Initial-state retrieval in development was 0.984. Gemma reads these records
but does not apply the policy reliably, so no confirmation split was generated for it. This is an
eligibility result: it says nothing for or against stale-state interference in Gemma. The conclusions
above therefore rest on one model.
