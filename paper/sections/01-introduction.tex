\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Conversations and documents often update a fact while leaving its earlier version in view. A user's preference changes, an object moves, a task variable receives a new value. A language model can answer with the current value and still be influenced by the old one: its scores for candidate answers may shift when the old value changes, even though the top answer does not. This paper asks what governs that residual sensitivity.

Consider a history from our main experiment:

\begin{prompt}
Previously, Nora’s badge was amber.
Previously, Liam’s badge was coral.
Currently, Nora’s badge is jade.
Currently, Liam’s badge is pearl.
What is Nora’s current badge?
Respond with only the value, with no explanation.
\end{prompt}

The correct answer is \lit{jade}. Suppose we replace the earlier \lit{amber} with \lit{violet} and leave the other prompt content fixed. The edit can shift the model's scores for \lit{violet} relative to \lit{amber}. If the shift is larger when the question asks about Nora than when it asks about Liam, the score change is query-specific. We call the difference between these two shifts \emph{query relevance}, $R$ (Section~\ref{sec:edits}).

A positive $R$ for the history above could arise for several reasons. The model may represent that \lit{amber} \emph{was} Nora's badge, a superseded assignment to the queried attribute. It may associate \lit{amber} with Nora because they appear in the same sentence, or link them by position because both come first in their blocks. The explicit marker \lit{Previously} could also affect the score contrast. We compare prompt constructions that hold some features fixed while changing others, and cross them with historical and current entity order. The contrasts constrain possible explanations but do not independently identify each factor.

In the two models we test, Qwen3-8B and Gemma 3 4B, \textbf{query-specific score sensitivity to a historical value depends more on how and where the value is mentioned than on whether it was ever assigned to the queried attribute.} Replacing an updated value of the queried attribute by a never-updated value of another attribute, with the sentence otherwise identical, changes $R$ on average by 0.314 nats in Qwen and 0.004 in Gemma; a follow-up shows that Qwen's small average combines a positive effect of having been the queried attribute and a negative effect of having been updated. Mentioning the value in a note about the entity instead raises $R$ by 3.504 nats in Qwen and 2.589 in Gemma as Experiment 1 constructs the two prompts, and by 1.293 and 0.367 nats once the marker and the name--value distance are matched. Values that are mentioned but assigned to no one are credited to whichever entity occupies the same relative position, so their effect reverses sign when the order of mentions is reversed. The marker \lit{Previously} lowers $R$ in both constructions where it was tested. The direction of the finer effects differs between the models: reversing mention order raises entity-mention relevance in Qwen but lowers it in Gemma, and the marker's effect depends on construction in Qwen but not detectably in Gemma. A follow-up shows that placing the value next to the entity name does not explain why entity mentions are more influential than superseded assignments.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A counterfactual measure of query-specific sensitivity to a historical value, applied to six prompt constructions crossed with independently varied historical and current mention order (Sections~\ref{sec:task}--\ref{sec:setup}).
\item A comparison of the prompt features that change this sensitivity: relation status contributes little on average, while construction and mention order change it more, with some directions specific to the model (Section~\ref{sec:main-findings}, Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}).
\item Order-stratified evidence that never-assigned values are credited by position, with effects that cancel when orders are averaged (Section~\ref{sec:order}).
\item A 96-history test of the marker \lit{Previously} and four follow-ups: a control that separates having been the queried attribute from having been updated, a name--value distance manipulation, update chains, and additional models. In Qwen the relation-status average hides a positive and a negative effect, and sensitivity grows with the number of updates while answers stay correct (Sections~\ref{sec:marker-results} and~\ref{sec:followups}).
\end{enumerate}
