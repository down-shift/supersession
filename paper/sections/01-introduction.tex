\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Conversations and documents often update a fact while leaving its earlier version in view. A user's preference changes, an object moves, a task variable receives a new value. A language model can answer with the current value and still be influenced by the old one: its scores for candidate answers may shift when the old value changes, even though the top answer does not. This paper asks what governs that residual sensitivity.

Consider a history from our main experiment:

\begin{prompt}
Previously, Nora’s badge was amber.
Previously, Liam’s badge was coral.
Currently, Nora’s badge is jade.
Currently, Liam’s badge is pearl.
What is Nora’s current badge?
Respond with only the value, with no explanation.
\end{prompt}

The correct answer is \lit{jade}. Suppose we replace the earlier \lit{amber} with \lit{violet} and leave the other prompt content fixed. The edit can shift the model's scores for \lit{violet} relative to \lit{amber}. If the shift is larger when the question asks about Nora than when it asks about Liam, the score change is query-specific. We call the difference between these two shifts \emph{query relevance}, $R$ (Section~\ref{sec:edits}).

A positive $R$ for the history above could arise for several reasons. The model may represent that \lit{amber} \emph{was} Nora's badge, a superseded assignment to the queried attribute. It may associate \lit{amber} with Nora because they appear in the same sentence, or link them by position because both come first in their blocks. The explicit marker \lit{Previously} could also affect the score contrast. We compare prompt constructions that hold some features fixed while changing others, and cross them with historical and current entity order. The contrasts constrain possible explanations but do not independently identify each factor.

In seven models from five families, \textbf{query-specific score sensitivity to a historical value depends more on where and how the value is mentioned than on whether it was ever assigned to the queried attribute.} Reversing mention order changes $R$ for a never-assigned value by 4.970 to 11.837 nats in every model, more than relation status changes it in any model, which ranges from $-1.305$ nats in Mistral-7B to 1.162 in Qwen3-14B. Mentioning the value with the entity instead of as a superseded assignment raises $R$ in six of the seven models; in Mistral-7B the difference is unresolved and its sign depends on mention order. That gap exceeds relation status in five models, not resolvably in Granite-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

We study two models most closely, Qwen3-8B and Gemma 3 4B. In them, replacing an updated value of the queried attribute by a never-updated value of another attribute, with the sentence otherwise identical, changes $R$ on average by 0.314 nats in Qwen and 0.004 in Gemma; a separate follow-up with different prompts finds, in Qwen, a positive effect of queried-attribute status when both assignments are updated and a negative effect of updating when the assignment concerns another attribute, consistent with the two partly cancelling. Mentioning the value in a note about the entity instead raises $R$ by 3.504 nats in Qwen and 2.589 in Gemma as Experiment 1 constructs the two prompts, and by 1.293 and 0.367 nats once the marker and the name--value distance are matched. Values that are mentioned but assigned to no one are credited to whichever entity occupies the same relative position, so their effect reverses sign when the order of mentions is reversed. The marker \lit{Previously} lowers $R$ in both constructions where it was tested. The direction of the finer effects differs between the models: reversing mention order raises entity-mention relevance in Qwen but lowers it in Gemma, and the marker-by-construction interaction is positive in Qwen, while in Gemma the pooled interaction is unresolved because the two order groups have opposing, resolved interactions. The two models narrowly excluded by the competence screen show the same ordering.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A counterfactual measure of query-specific sensitivity to a historical value, applied to six prompt constructions crossed with independently varied historical and current mention order (Sections~\ref{sec:task}--\ref{sec:setup}).
\item A comparison of the prompt features that change this sensitivity: relation status contributes little on average in the two main models and at most a quarter of superseded relevance in any model, while construction and mention order change it more, with some directions specific to the model (Section~\ref{sec:main-findings}, Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition}).
\item Order-stratified evidence that never-assigned values are credited by position, with effects that cancel when orders are averaged (Section~\ref{sec:order}).
\item A 96-history test of the marker \lit{Previously} and four follow-ups: a control that varies whether the other attribute is also updated, a name--value distance manipulation, update chains, and five additional screened models, which extend the comparison to seven models from five families. In Qwen a separate update-control design finds a positive attribute effect and a negative update effect; in a development run of update chains every generated answer was correct, and in an independent score-only run Qwen's sensitivity to the immediately superseded value grows with chain depth (Sections~\ref{sec:marker-results}--\ref{sec:additional-models}).
\end{enumerate}
