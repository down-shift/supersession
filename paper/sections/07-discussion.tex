\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A model's sensitivity to an earlier value tracks how that value is attached to an entity in the text more than what the value used to mean. A superseded value of the queried attribute and a never-updated value of another attribute differ by about a third of a nat or less in Qwen3-8B and Gemma 3 4B and by $-1.305$ to 1.162 nats in the other screened models, although only the first is contradicted by the current state. In six of the seven models a value placed in a sentence with the entity, even a note described as unrelated, produces more query-specific sensitivity than a superseded assignment, and a value attached to no one is credited to whichever entity shares its position. For the question that motivated this work, whether models retain obsolete assignments, the residual sensitivity is not specific to obsolescence, at least in the averages and directions the models share (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Beyond position, the ranking has exceptions. In Granite-3.1-8B the entity-mention gap and relation status are of similar size. In Mistral-7B the construction difference changes sign with mention order and superseded values count less than other-attribute values. An update discount larger than the positive queried-attribute effect could produce this pattern. Qwen's follow-up establishes partial cancellation, not dominance of the update effect; neither explanation was tested in Mistral, and Mistral's negative estimate becomes unresolved when histories with ranking errors are excluded. In the larger Qwen and Gemma models relation status is resolved and positive.

Within Qwen and Gemma, the directions of the finer effects also differ: reversing mention order raises entity-mention relevance in Qwen and lowers it in Gemma, and the marker-by-construction interaction is positive in Qwen in both order groups but reverses sign between order groups in Gemma. The data therefore support a claim about which prompt features carry weight, not a single rule that predicts their direction in every model. The positional result connects these short prompts to findings in longer ones: \citet{liu2024lost} and \citet{yang2026conflicts} report that where evidence appears changes how models use it. Prior work identifies abstract binding IDs \citep{feng2024bind}, ordering IDs \citep{prakash2026lookbacks}, and positional, lexical, and reflexive retrieval mechanisms \citep{gurarieh2026mixing}. Our prompt-level results do not distinguish among these mechanisms. For evaluation, averaging over mention orders can produce null effects that conceal large opposing ones.

Why entity mentions outweigh superseded assignments remains open. The gap persists without the marker and, in the tested templates, when the name--value token gap is matched, although those rewrites also change syntax. The constructions still differ in predicate (\lit{mentioned} versus \lit{was}) and in whether the value is framed as an attribute at all.

Score changes on low-probability candidates expose residual sensitivity to the prompt while the current candidate stays dominant, which answer accuracy cannot show. The matched controls then test whether that sensitivity is specific to obsolete assignments. Magnitudes in log score do not translate into practical error rates.

\paragraph{Limitations.} First, the competence screen is an admission criterion on a separate set of 24 histories; confirmation-set accuracy is reported separately and falls below the screening level for Mistral-7B (Section~\ref{sec:additional-models}). Second, model size: at the sizes tested (4B to 14B parameters), position exceeds relation status in every model and construction does in five of seven, and within both families the larger model gives relation status more weight, an observation from two sizes per family, not a scaling trend; whether relation status overtakes construction or position in larger models is untested and would qualify the main claim. Third, all results concern the tested implementations with int8 weights; native precision was not evaluated. Fourth, the measure is narrow by design: scores are bounded masses of fixed answer prefixes, and the edited candidates have low absolute scores, so their changes do not imply that probability moves between them or that a generated error becomes likely. Fifth, the models are at or near ceiling in candidate ranking (Table~\ref{tab:c-accuracy}). In Qwen and Gemma, neither appended distractor entities (Appendix~\ref{app:harder}) nor the development run of update chains up to depth 5 produced a generated answer naming an earlier value, and the 96-history chain run scored candidates only. These generation runs did not reveal stale-value answers; whether the measured score sensitivity predicts generated-answer changes remains unestablished. OLMo-2, an exploratory model with lower accuracy, shows a similar qualitative pattern in its generated answers, but its own candidate scores order the constructions differently. The derived-code task (Appendix~\ref{app:derived-code}) brings Qwen's candidate ranking below ceiling but lacks the Experiment 1 controls and generated answers; a confirmatory test would need a screened model below ceiling, or a task with those controls that brings Qwen and Gemma there, and generated answers. Finally, names, attributes, templates, vocabularies, the language, and the models are fixed or narrowly sampled, so the intervals do not quantify generalization beyond these stimuli.
