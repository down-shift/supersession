\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A model's sensitivity to an earlier value tracks how that value is attached to an entity in the text more than what the value used to mean. A value that was the queried attribute and has since been updated, and a value that was some other attribute of the same entity and was never updated, differ on average by about a third of a nat or less in Qwen3-8B and Gemma 3 4B, and by at most 1.162 nats in the other screened models, although only the first is contradicted by the current state. A value placed in a sentence with the entity, even a note described as unrelated, is more influential than either: by 3.504 and 2.589 nats in Qwen and Gemma as Experiment 1 constructs the prompts, and by 1.293 and 0.367 nats with the marker and name--value distance matched (Table~\ref{tab:gap}). A value attached to no one is credited to whichever entity shares its position. So for the question that motivated this work, whether models retain obsolete assignments, these scores say that the residual sensitivity is not specific to obsolescence, at least in the averages and directions the models share (Table~\ref{tab:models}). The ranking is not universal in every detail: in Granite-3.1-8B the entity-mention gap and relation status are of similar size, while its positional effect is the largest of any model, and in the larger Qwen and Gemma models relation status is resolved and positive. The prespecified relation-selective hypothesis failed, but that failure does not mean relation status has no effect. Separated in the follow-up, having been the queried attribute raises Qwen's relevance by 0.740 nats while being contradicted by an update lowers it by 0.289; the small joint average reflects these opposing effects. In Gemma 3 4B both are small. The follow-up was not run in Gemma 3 12B, but its joint relation-status contrast is resolved and positive, 0.442 [0.249, 0.631] nats, about a tenth of its superseded relevance (Section~\ref{sec:additional-models}).

The shared regularities sit alongside model-specific ones. Both models credit unattached values by position, and both give entity mentions more weight than superseded assignments on average. But reversing mention order raises entity-mention relevance in Qwen and lowers it in Gemma; the marker reduces entity-mention relevance more than superseded relevance in Qwen, but by similar amounts in Gemma; and in Gemma's reversed-order prompts without the marker, the difference between entity mentions and superseded assignments is not resolved. An exploratory Phi-4-mini run showed a third marker pattern. The data therefore support a claim about which prompt features carry weight, not a single rule that predicts their direction in every model. The positional result connects these short prompts to findings in longer ones: \citet{liu2024lost} and \citet{yang2026conflicts} report that where evidence appears changes how models use it, and positional binding of entities \citep{feng2024bind,prakash2026lookbacks} becomes one of several retrieval mechanisms, and an unreliable one, in longer contexts \citep{gurarieh2026mixing}. A practical consequence for evaluation is that averaging over mention orders can produce null effects that conceal large opposing ones.

Why entity mentions outweigh superseded assignments remains open. The gap persists without the marker, so it is not a marker effect, and the distance follow-up rules out name--value proximity as the main cause: placing the name next to the value raised superseded relevance but not entity-mention relevance, and a gap remained at matched distance. The constructions still differ in predicate (\lit{mentioned} versus \lit{was}) and in whether the value is framed as an attribute at all. The update follow-up tested one candidate explanation, that an assignment followed by an update is discounted while a free mention is not. In Qwen an update does lower relevance, by 0.289 nats, about a fifth of the 1.293-nat gap that remains with marker and distance matched; in Gemma no discount is detectable. Most of the remaining gap is not explained by the properties tested here. Experiment 2 tested the literal prefix \lit{Previously,}, not temporal information as such.

\paragraph{Limitations.} First, model size. Within both families the larger model gives relation status more weight and the entity-mention gap less (Section~\ref{sec:additional-models}). At the sizes tested (4B to 14B parameters), construction and position exceed relation status in every model but Granite-3.1-8B; whether relation status overtakes construction in larger models is untested and would qualify the main claim. Second, the measure is narrow by design. Scores are bounded masses of fixed answer prefixes, not probabilities of generated answers, and the edited source and donor candidates have low absolute scores; their changes do not imply that probability moves between them or that a generated error becomes likely. The models answer at ceiling: the current value is top-ranked in every superseded trial, and two attempts to lower accuracy failed. With two to six appended distractor entities (Appendix~\ref{app:harder}) and with update chains of up to five values (Section~\ref{sec:depth}), neither model ever answered a superseded prompt with the earlier value, even as Qwen's candidate-score sensitivity rose by 4.482 nats from three to five updates. In the screened models, then, the score shifts do not reach answers. An exploratory study of OLMo-2, which is below ceiling and outside the screened population, suggests what happens when they can: its errors follow the same ordering, with mentioned and position-aligned values pulling answers toward the donor and superseded values almost never surfacing. A confirmatory test would need a screened model below ceiling or a task that brings Qwen and Gemma there. Histories are the bootstrap unit, and names, attributes, templates, the eight- or twelve-value vocabularies, English, and the models are fixed or narrowly sampled, so the intervals do not quantify generalization beyond these stimuli.
